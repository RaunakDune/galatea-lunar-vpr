\Cref{tab:ablation} varies one factor at a time. Single-seed differences below about two points
should be read against the seed standard deviation of roughly one point (\cref{tab:main}).

\paragraph*{Assignment relaxation}
Every value of $\lambda$ gives the same accuracy within seed noise. R@1 is $\lamZeroRone\%$ at
$\lambda=0$ (three seeds), $\galRone\%$ with learned $\lambda$, and $\salRone\%$ at $\lambda=1$. When
$\lambda$ is learned, it barely leaves its initialisation ($\bar\lambda=\galLam$). Even with a
$25\times$ larger learning rate on $\lambda$ and $z$ it only reaches $\galFastLam$, at unchanged
accuracy ($\galFastRone\%$). Both observations follow from \cref{prop:colinv}. Once descriptors
are intra-normalised, $\lambda$ acts only through the token weights $w_i$, a second-order effect that
yields a weak training signal. The practical implication is that the cheaper $\lambda=0$ assignment
(a single softmax, no Sinkhorn loop) is as good as balanced OT for this task.

\paragraph*{Dust-bin, iterations and clusters}
Removing the dust-bin does not hurt here ($\noDustRone\%$ vs.\ $\galSzeroRone\%$, single seed). In
LunarSim the uninformative sky is near-black, so its tokens are already near-constant and cheap to
ignore. This may not hold for real imagery with stray light or lens flare. Changing the number of Sinkhorn iterations ($L=1$: $\LoneRone\%$, $L=10$: $\LtenRone\%$) moves R@1
by less than seed noise, in line with the fast contraction of \cref{prop:contraction}. Halving the
clusters to $m=32$ ($4352$-D) costs nothing ($\mThirtytwoRone\%$), which is attractive for
bandwidth-limited rovers. With $m=16$ ($2304$-D), R@1 drops to $\mSixteenRone\%$.

\paragraph*{Illumination-diverse training}
By far the largest effect comes from the training data. We re-rendered the five training scenes
with the \emph{same} poses but a single Sun direction (that of the database). Galatea then reaches
only $\fixIllRone\%$ R@1, $\fixIllDrop$ points below illumination-diverse training, and SALAD drops
the same way ($\salFixIllRone\%$). The single-illumination model is slightly \emph{better} when the
query light matches its training light (C0: $\fixIllCzero\%$ vs.\ $\galSzeroCzero\%$), but on the
three hardest conditions it falls to $\fixIllHardVal\%$, against $\galSzeroHard\%$. For lunar VPR, an
illumination sweep in the training data, as provided by renderers such as LunarSim or DEM-based
pipelines, matters far more than the choice of aggregation layer.
