\section{Discussion: What Matters for Lunar VPR}\label{sec:discussion}
% ======================================================================
Viewing both stages as OT problems lets us separate three kinds of design decision and rank them by
their measured effect on LunarSim-VPR. Two choices are prerequisites rather than levers: training on
lunar imagery at all ($+\gainZsMean$ points over the best zero-shot model) and cluster-based rather
than globally pooled aggregation (more than $20$ points). The ranking below assumes both.

\paragraph*{Training data first}
Illumination diversity in training is worth $\fixIllDrop$ points of R@1 for the Galatea head with a
frozen backbone and $\ftFixIllDrop$ with a fine-tuned one, more than fine-tuning adds to the same head.
A more adaptable model fits a single training light more closely, so illumination diversity and
fine-tuning have to go together.

\paragraph*{Then backbone adaptation, then aggregation}
Fine-tuning the last four blocks adds $11$--$19$ points for every head. Among the cluster-based heads,
the aggregation design matters more than the assignment: residual NetVLAD aggregation differs from its
closest OT relative by $\famFrNvVsNDMean$ points on frozen tokens and by $\famFtNvVsNDMean$ once the
backbone is trained (NetVLAD minus OT). Within the OT family the assignment is a weak design lever:
with a dust-bin, all values of $\lambda$ are equivalent on a frozen backbone (P1, P2), and on
a fine-tuned backbone the fixed members stay within $\famFtLamRange$ points of each other while a
learned $\lambda$ behaves like a fixed $\lambda$ at its initial value. The cheapest
member, $\lambda=0$, needs no Sinkhorn loop and matches SALAD's balanced assignment within seed noise. A
learnable $\lambda$ adds a parameter that the loss barely constrains (P2), so it should be fixed;
$\lambda=0$ is the natural choice for speed, and no fixed value is clearly better than another.

\paragraph*{Fusion, not the matcher}
Local evidence on the Moon is abundant but unreliable, because shadow edges dominate keypoint
detections. Its value lies in queries where the global ranking is uncertain, and under the fusion rule
the evidence needed to overturn a global decision grows linearly with the global margin
(\cref{prop:override}). With fusion in place, a training-free OT matcher, plain mutual nearest
neighbours and a learned terrestrial matcher perform alike, so the cheapest adequate scorer,
MNN+MAGSAC++, is a sensible default (LightGlue costs $\approx\tLgK$\,ms and FGW $\approx\tFgwK$\,ms per
query on a CPU); FGW earns its cost mainly where no learned matcher is available or trusted.

\paragraph*{What the theory did and did not predict}
Predictions (P1)--(P3) held for fixed tokens with a dust-bin. The ordering of heads with trained tokens,
including the advantage of residual aggregation, is an empirical finding on simulated data.
